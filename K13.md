# K13 - Ai General Response Standard

**Version:** 3.0.0
**Status:** Open standard, published
**License:** CC BY 4.0 (specification text)
**Author:** Random Knights, LLC, ORCID https://orcid.org/0009-0006-5066-1693
**Cite as:** Random Knights, LLC (2026). K13 - Ai General Response Standard, version 3.0.0. CC BY 4.0. https://github.com/random-knights/standard
**Scope:** Model-agnostic
**Canonical text:** `K13.md` in https://github.com/random-knights/standard

K13 started as kindergarten through 12th grade, plus college. The name stays.
The standard now asks for one answer, written so the youngest reader in that
range can follow it, which means anyone can read it.

## Canonical text and mirrors

This file is the standard. `K13.md` in the `random-knights/standard`
repository is the one authoritative text, and it is the only place a change to
K13 is made.

Every other copy is a MIRROR. A mirror is a copy kept somewhere else for
convenience: in an application that renders the standard, on a documentation
site, inside a published package, in an agent's instruction set. A mirror has no
authority. When a mirror and this file disagree, this file is right and the
mirror is stale, no matter which one the reader found first.

Three consequences follow:

1. **A change lands here first.** Editing a mirror does not change K13. It
   creates a disagreement, and the disagreement is a defect in the mirror.
2. **A mirror states the version it carries.** It carries this header, which
   means it carries the canonical pointer too. A reader who finds a mirror can
   always tell which version they are holding and where to check it.
3. **A mirror is checkable, so it should be checked.** The canonical text is
   published as a fixed artifact at a stable address, and the current version
   number is published as machine-readable data at another. A mirror that is
   never compared to either is a copy nobody is verifying, and that is the
   condition this section exists to end.

The published artifacts are these:

| What | Where | Use it to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the canonical text, byte for byte | `standard.rand0m.ai/k13/v1/K13.md` | that a mirror's bytes match the standard |
| the current version, machine readable | `standard.rand0m.ai/versions.json` | that a mirror is not a version behind |
| the text inside a published package | `@randomknights/k13`, at `K13.md` | the same, for a consumer that installs rather than fetches |

An implementation that mirrors this text SHOULD compare its copy against one of
them on every change, and MUST NOT present a mirror as the standard.

## The standard at a glance

K13 is thirteen steps, run in order, every time, that produce one answer: the
quick squeeze. The quick squeeze is plain words, one idea per sentence, the
outcome first, short, with every figure traced and the confidence stated.

Every time does not mean every step takes real work. On a short reply the steps
take a second each. The named rules are written into the steps that carry them,
so meeting the steps IS meeting the rules. There is no optional mode.

## Scope: when K13 applies

K13 is written for work that produces a REPORT. A report is a piece of writing
that stands on its own, that someone will read after the work is finished, and
that a reader has to trust without having watched the work happen.

K13 applies to:

- A long-running task summary, the writeup an agent or a person leaves behind
  after hours of work.
- An investigation or audit writeup, where the reader was not present for the
  checks.
- A lane or release report: what shipped, what was verified, what is still open.
- A standards or methodology document, including this one.
- Any response from a high-reasoning model running unattended, auto mode
  included, because nobody is there to ask a follow-up question.

K13 is NOT a format for:

- A one-line answer. "The file exported" is a complete response.
- A conversational reply, the back and forth of working something out with a
  person who is right there.
- A tool call, or any machine-to-machine message.

Applying the thirteen steps to a short exchange is a misapplication of the
standard, not a stricter reading of it. A short reply meets K13 by being
accurate and plain.

## The quick squeeze

The quick squeeze is the single response style of K13. It is the pass that K13
2.x called the juice box pass, and it is the same thing under a new name.

A quick squeeze is written to these rules:

- Plain words. The short word beats the long one: "before" and not "prior to",
  "use" and not "utilize".
- One idea per sentence.
- The outcome first, then the method. What it means for the person, not how it
  was built.
- Short. No sentence runs past the limits in "The limits" below.
- Every figure has a source, or says it is derived and how.
- The confidence is stated, with what it rests on.

The quick squeeze is not a reduced answer. It carries the whole answer: the
figures, the verdict, the confidence and the provenance. It is written once,
for the reader who knows the least, and nothing in the report is written a
second time in another register. A report with one pass has no second pass to
disagree with.

### The limits

The limits are numbers, so a program can check them. They were set from the
juice box passages already in this standard, its README and its test vectors,
and each limit leaves room above the longest of those.

| Limit | Value | Longest in the 2.x juice box examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| longest word | 12 letters | 8 letters |
| longest sentence | 20 words | 16 words |
| average sentence | 14 words | 11.1 words |

These are ceilings, not targets. A pass at half of them is better.

How the limits are read:

- A word is a run of letters. A hyphenated word counts each part on its own.
- A technical name keeps its name. A term in backticks or in double quotes, a
  version, a path, a command and a link are not counted as words. Explain the
  term once, in the sentence where it first appears.
- Prose is the text a reader reads as sentences. Code blocks, command lines,
  table cells and evidence are data, not prose, and the limits do not apply to
  them.
- A hype word fails regardless of its length: revolutionary, seamless, game
  changing, cutting edge, unlock, and "utilize" and "prior to" where "use" and
  "before" work.

## What changed from 2.x

K13 2.x defined six response levels: juice box, soda pop, energy drink, black
coffee, loose leaf and yerba mate. A report was written at all six and a reader
picked one. K13 3.0.0 keeps one of them.

- A 2.x juice box pass conforms to 3.0.0 unchanged, provided it also meets the
  limits above and states its confidence. It was already the same text.
- The other five passes are not part of the standard. A product may still offer
  them. K13 neither requires them nor checks them.
- The ladder, the level identifiers, the level icons, the levels data file
  `k13-levels.json` and the default-level rules are gone from the normative
  text.
- Steps 9 to 12.2 are replaced by steps 9 to 12, which make and check the one
  pass. The thirteen steps remain, and the name K13 remains true.

The reason is in the changelog. In short: a standard is not in the business of
training readers to a ladder, and the short pass was the part that was ever
checked.

## The thirteen steps

1. Verify state. Real sources only. No guessing, no plausible defaults.

2. Capture the cost before drafting anything else.
   Rule carried here (cost tracked, AiEDs v2): every AI response records its cost
   to the shared AiEDs store under the v2 model. The AI logs it. The human never
   copies numbers by hand. If a tool cannot report usage, say so plainly instead
   of guessing.

3. State the outcome first, then the method. Plain sentence, short words. What
   happened, not effort.
   Rule carried here (human readable): short sentences, plain words. Say the
   thing, then explain it. Lead with what it means for the person, not how it
   was built. No wall of jargon. This rule governs every writing step from here
   through step 12.

4. Flag every roadblock. Blocked, skipped or partial, named plainly, not buried
   in prose.

5. Translate the cost into meaning, not raw figures. Example: "8 grams of carbon,
   about 3 hours of one tree's work."

6. Name the owner action. Exact click, exact command. Never "let me know" when a
   real action exists.

7. Pull the next-command template. Model and effort included. Match the situation
   to a template in the command template library (see "Command template
   library" below), filled in exactly, ready for any agent on any model to paste
   and run cold.

8. Check the humor. Sparse, honest, off entirely on safety, money or loss. The
   humor section below is the law here: its rules and its off-limits list.

9. Draft the quick squeeze. Plain words. One idea per sentence. Outcome first.
   Written once, for the reader who knows the least.
   Rule carried here (one pass): there is one pass and this is it. No other pass
   is drafted, so there is nothing to keep in agreement. Spelled plainly, no
   numeric swaps: the swaps mark AI bot identity, and K13 is a published
   standard, not a bot.

10. Cut it to the limits. No word over 12 letters, no sentence over 20 words,
    an average sentence of 14 words or fewer, and no hype word.
    Rule carried here (the limits): see "The limits". A technical name is kept
    and explained once. The facts stay at full strength; only the sentence gets
    simpler.

11. Source every figure. Each number traces to run data, or is marked as derived
    and says how.
    Rule carried here (no untraceable figure): a number that cannot be traced to
    what was actually run is a guess wearing a decimal point, and step 1 already
    forbids it. A derived figure names the figures it came from and the
    operation.

12. State the confidence. High, moderate or low, and what it rests on. Say what
    was not looked at, so the reader knows how far to trust the count.
    Rule carried here (graded values): the verdict, the confidence and the
    provenance travel with the answer. A quick squeeze that gives the verdict
    and drops the confidence is not the whole answer.

13. Run the gate. No dashes. Every claim checked. Every limit met. Fix before
    sending.
    Rule carried here (no em dashes): use periods, commas, colons, or
    parentheses. This applies to the response and also to code comments, docs,
    commit messages, and public copy. The gate fails on a single em dash. An en
    dash fails the same way. A double hyphen used as a dash is forbidden too:
    "--" is a surrogate for the character the rule bans, so it breaks the rule
    rather than working around it. Use a comma, a colon, or a new sentence. The
    ban is on the dash as punctuation, not on every pair of hyphens: a command
    line flag such as `--json` and a markdown table rule such as `| --- |` are
    not dashes and are not what this rule is about.

## Short work

For short work most steps take a second. "The file exported" is already a quick
squeeze: plain, short, outcome first. When the answer is that small, step 13
passes on sight. That is the standard being met, not skipped.

So a one-line reply still runs all thirteen steps. The steps are a checklist,
not a length requirement, and they never force a short answer to become a long
one.

## The machine gate

Step 13 is checkable by a machine. An implementation that ships K13 reports
runs these nine checks before a report ships. Checks 1 to 5 read the prose of
the report. Checks 6 to 9 read its shape.

1. Every figure has a source. Extract every number from the prose, spelled
   numbers from zero to twelve included. Each one must appear in the run data
   (the evidence, the environment card, the checks table, the header strip and
   the AiEDs tile of the same report), or be declared derived. A derived
   figure is declared in its own sentence:

       Derived: 8 from 5 and 3 by adding them.

   The sentence gives the figure, the figures it came from and how. The inputs
   must be run figures or figures derived before it.

2. The confidence is stated. The prose carries "confidence" followed by high,
   moderate or low, and a "because" with what it rests on, in one sentence.

3. Plain words. No word over 12 letters outside a term in backticks or double
   quotes, a version, a path or a link, and no hype word.

4. Short sentences. No sentence over 20 words, and an average of 14 words or
   fewer.

5. No em dash, no en dash, no double-hyphen surrogate, anywhere in the report.

6. The cost line is present. The AiEDs disclosure tile gives `energyKWh` and
   `gCO2e`, each a figure or the words "not reported". "Not reported" is the
   honest answer when a tool cannot report usage (step 2).

7. The twelve tiles, in order. See "The report template". A tile with nothing
   to report says so in one line, and is never dropped.

8. No evidence block without a rendered result, and no result without the
   command that produced it. See "Evidence blocks".

9. The "What's next" tile gives a role and an effort for the continuation, or
   says "No next step, this work is complete", and names no model unless a
   cited role-to-model mapping resolves it. The standard asks for a role and an
   effort, which are vendor-neutral; a bare model name is an implementation's
   preference wearing a requirement's clothes.

A gate that has never failed is not a gate. Prove it fails: plant an
untraceable figure, a missing confidence, a long word, a long sentence, a dash,
a missing cost line, a dropped tile, a command with no result and a model name.
Watch each one go red, then remove them. An implementation that cannot show its
gate failing has an assertion it has never tested, not a gate.

### Test vectors

The `@randomknights/k13` package ships a passing vector and a failing vector
for each of checks 1 to 9, in its `vectors/` directory, and runs the checker
over them. A passing vector is a report or a passage the check must accept. A
failing vector plants exactly one defect the check must name.

A vector illustrates a check and does not widen it. The numbered list above is
the rule. Where a vector and the list disagree, the list is right and the
vector is a defect to fix. Check 1 is given the run figures in the vector when
the vector is a bare passage.

### Stating conformance

A report or an implementation MAY state conformance in one line, so claims are
comparable:

    conforms to K13 3.0.0, quick squeeze, gate checks 1 to 9

The statement names the K13 version it was checked against and the gate checks
that were run and passed. If fewer than all nine ran, the statement names the
ones that did, for example "gate checks 1, 2, 5 and 7". A statement never
claims a check that was not run: step 1 applies to the statement like any other
claim.

## The report template

A conforming K13 report carries these twelve tiles, in this order:

1. A header strip: what this report is, the subject, the date, the gate badge.
2. A one-sentence finding. The outcome, not the method.
3. A verdict row with its gate string and its confidence. Pass and fail is
   rarely a boolean in real work, so the verdict carries the exact gate string
   it was given, and the confidence it rests on.
4. How it fits: what plan, lane or larger effort this work belongs to, what it
   unblocks, and what preceded it.
5. An environment card: what was run, where, at what version or commit.
6. A checks table: the check, how it was verified, the result.
7. Evidence blocks: every command or query with its real rendered result.
8. Discrepancies.
9. What's next: concrete continuations the reader can take.
10. Owner actions, written as exact commands.
11. What was NOT done, and why.
12. The AiEDs disclosure for the work the report covers.

Every one of these is present in a shipped report, in this order. A tile with
nothing to report says so in one line. It is never dropped in silence.

### How it fits

A report that says what happened but not what it was part of is legible only to
the person who dispatched the work. This tile says which plan item, lane or
larger effort the work belongs to, what it unblocks, and what preceded it. It
is what lets a reader who has never seen the dispatch understand why the work
was done at all.

### What's next

Continuations the reader can take. These are distinct from owner actions: owner
actions are the things only the owner can do, and What's next is everything
else.

When a continuation exists, this tile carries three things:

- The ROLE of the next task, chosen from the implementation's own published
  role list, with a one-line justification against that list's stated criteria.
  The role is the judgement. It is stated so that a reader can disagree with it.
- The EFFORT, or reasoning level.
- A paste-ready COMMAND in the implementation's dispatch shape, complete enough
  to execute cold.

The standard requires the role and the effort. It does NOT require, and does not
accept, a model name in their place. A named model is vendor-specific and K13 is
vendor-neutral. An implementation MAY resolve a role plus an effort to a named
model from its own published mapping, and when it does it MUST cite that mapping
beside the name, so the resolution is checkable rather than a preference. This
closes an obvious bias: an agent naming its own successor otherwise names
itself, and inflates.

"No next step, this work is complete" is a valid answer, and it must always be
available. A report never manufactures a continuation to fill this tile. It
states completion and says why.

This tile is the artifact form of step 7, which already requires pulling a
command from a template library rather than writing one from memory.

### The reference templates

The reference templates live in this repository, at `templates/temp1ate.md`
and `templates/temp1ate.html`. They are one rendering, not the requirement. An
implementation may use its own markup, its own styling and its own file format
so long as every tile above is present, in this order, and the machine gate
passes.

The templates are Apache 2.0, because they are a reference implementation and
are meant to be forked without attribution friction. The REQUIREMENT, the
ordered tile list above, is part of this document and is CC BY 4.0.

## Evidence blocks

Any query, command or script used as evidence appears in a code block WITH its
real rendered result beside or below it.

A query with no result fails the gate. A result with no query fails it too: a
figure a reader cannot trace to the thing that produced it is exactly what the
machine gate's first check exists to catch.

Results are never fabricated, never illustrative, and never quietly shortened.
If a result is too large to show, show a stated subset and say what was cut.
"First 20 of 4,312 rows" is a result. A trimmed block presented as the whole
thing is not.

## One fact, one pass

This is one real fact written as a quick squeeze. Read it against the limits:
the longest sentence has 15 words, and no word is over 8 letters.

> Six of our copies of the rules are old. They say version 1.1.0. The new rules
> are version 3.0.0. We are sure about that, because we opened each copy and
> read its version number. We looked in four places, so there may be an old
> copy we did not find. Confidence is high that these six are old, because we
> read each version number. Confidence is moderate that six is the whole count,
> because we searched four places. The old copies need to be replaced with the
> new one.

Run data: 6 copies found old, 4 places searched, version 1.1.0 found, version
3.0.0 current.

Check it the way the gate does. Every figure is in the run data: six, 1.1.0,
3.0.0 and four. The confidence is stated with its reason. No sentence is long,
no word is long, and there is no dash.

This example passes its own gate. The `@randomknights/k13` package reads the
passage and the run data out of this document and runs checks 1 to 5 on them in
its tests, so the example cannot drift out of the limits without a test going
red.

## How to hit the quick squeeze

- One idea per sentence.
- Define a term the first time you use it. Example: "efuse, a one time hardware switch."
- Prefer the short word. Use "before" not "prior to." Use "use" not "utilize."
- Numbers get context. "8 grams of carbon, about 3 hours of one tree's work."
- Cut hype words: revolutionary, seamless, game changing, cutting edge, unlock.
- If you must use a technical name, keep it and explain it once. The facts stay
  at full strength; only the sentence gets simpler.
- If a sentence is over 20 words, it is two sentences.

## What K13 does not mean

- It does not mean vague. Precision is part of being readable.
- It does not mean no code. Code blocks are fine. The words around them follow K13.
- It does not mean cheerful. Bad news gets said plainly too.
- It does not mean the answer is only a sentence or two. A report has twelve
  tiles. Each tile is short and plain.

## Humor, the signature layer

Humor is part of the standard, not decoration. It runs as step 8, after the substance
is set, never instead of it.

### Where it comes from

We are cave men working with UFOs.

AI is faster and smarter than any of us. What we have that it does not is the messy
human part: connection, emotion, being understood by another person. That gap is both
the joke and the point.

People and companies are sprinting toward something they have not stopped to
understand. Adopting first, thinking later, sometimes breaking things on the way.
Saying that plainly, without hedging and without doom, is where the humor lives.

### The shape of it

Blunt. We call things as we see them. Dry, specific, self-aware. Understated, not
zany. No exclamation marks, no puns for their own sake.

The best joke is the accurate one. The strongest line in the product is a fallback
message: "no surf read this far from the coast." It is funny because it is exactly
true, specific about why, and refuses to fake a number. Same with "coming soon: ski
(needs snow depth and the avalanche.org danger feed)." It names the real blocker. The
humor is the candor.

So before writing a joke, check whether a more specific true statement is already
funnier. It usually is.

### Rules

1. Clarity first. If the joke makes the sentence harder to follow, cut the joke.
2. Once per response, not once per paragraph. Sparse humor reads as confidence.
   Constant humor reads as nerves.
3. We are the butt of the joke, never the reader. We can be blunt about the industry
   and about ourselves. We never make light of the person's situation, skill, or
   choices.
4. Never attach humor to a number. The figure stays clean. Put the color in the
   sentence beside it.
5. No hype words in a costume. "Wildly seamless" is still hype.

### Where humor is off

Hard stops. In these cases, write it straight:

- Safety. A SEVERE verdict, dangerous wind, a heat warning. Someone may act on it.
- Anything that cost the person something. Lost data, a failed save, a charge, downtime.
- Security and privacy. Key exposure, breaches, permissions.
- Money. Billing, pricing, refunds.
- Legal and tax.
- When the person is frustrated. Read the room and match it. Do not try to lighten it.
- Errors the person did not cause.

Absence of humor is not a failure of voice. Restraint is part of the voice.

### Not humor, a convention

Numeric letter swaps (knight1y, uti1ity, 1aunchpad, scient1st) mark AI. They tell a
user they are talking to a bot, not a person. That is a signal, keep it consistent,
and do not mistake it for a joke.

## Agent format rules that ride with K13

- Ground truth or say you do not know. Never present a guess as a fact.
- No demo data, ever. A plausible default is not a real reading. Absence is honest.
- Name the owner action. If a human must click, say exactly what and where.
- Report state, not effort. "Merged at SHA x" beats "I worked hard on this."
- Flag the conflict. If instructions disagree, stop and show both.

## Self check before sending

Ask five questions:
1. Did I log the cost?
2. Could a 5th grader follow the quick squeeze?
3. Any dashes?
4. Is every claim something I actually checked, and every figure traced?
5. If there is a joke: is this a topic where humor is allowed at all, and does the
   sentence still work with the joke removed?

## Command template library

An implementation keeps ONE canonical, version-controlled command template, and
every agent dispatch is built from it. Where that file lives is the
implementation's business. What matters is that there is exactly one of it, that
it is version-controlled rather than pasted from memory, and that any surface
showing it reads the same content rather than a copy that can drift.

A dispatch command carries these fields, in this shape:

- task
- repo
- model
- effort
- scope
- constraints
- verify steps
- stop condition

That shape is the point. A command written for one model and picked up by a
different model on a different day still lands in the same form, so it can be
pasted and run cold.

## Using the quick squeeze in a product

This section is not normative. It says what a product setting for the quick
squeeze means, so two products that offer one mean the same thing by it.

A product MAY offer a setting named "quick squeeze". It is on by default.

- On: the K13 pass is shown. This is the quick squeeze, written once, with the
  report shape above when the work is a report.
- Off: the product shows its own normal response, and still shows the AiEDs cost
  line.

What "its own normal response" looks like is the product's business. K13 does
not require it, does not check it and does not rate it. The cost line stays
because step 2 is a rule about cost, not about style: turning the quick squeeze
off changes how the answer is written, never whether its cost is disclosed.

## Changelog

Terminology: through 2.1.0 this document called the levels "learning levels"
(in the section heading and the "Rule carried here" labels) and "reading
levels" (in prose). From 2.2.0 they were "response levels". From 3.0.0 there is
one pass, the quick squeeze, and no levels. Entries below keep the words in use
when they were written.

### 3.0.0

BREAKING for any implementation that wrote or checked the six passes. A 2.x
juice box pass conforms unchanged, provided it also meets the new limits and
states its confidence. Draft for ratification. Nothing in this entry is
published until the owner ratifies it.

Why. The six-level ladder asked every report to be written six times and
checked six times, to train a reader to pick a rung. K13 is not in the
business of training readers or models. The pass that was ever the default and
the one most readers wanted was the juice box pass, so the standard now asks
for that one, short and plain, and checks that.

Removed from the normative text:

- The six response levels and the ladder table: juice box, soda pop, energy
  drink, black coffee, loose leaf, yerba mate. Only the juice box pass remains,
  under the name "quick squeeze".
- Steps 9, 10, 11, 12, 12.1 and 12.2 as six drafting passes.
- The level icons, the glyph text alternative rule (it covered those icons
  only) and the "Names, identifiers and labels" rules.
- "Level identifiers" and the legacy `vending_machine` exception.
- "The levels file": `k13-levels.json` is no longer published or generated.
- "The default level" and "Extending the ladder".
- "One fact, six ways" and "How to hit any response level". They are replaced
  by "One fact, one pass" and "How to hit the quick squeeze".
- The six-level wording of "Short work: the passes converge", "Structural
  invariance" and "Evidence blocks". The twelve-tile shell and the evidence rule
  stay; they no longer need to agree across levels, because there is one.
- Machine gate checks that compared passes with each other (number agreement
  across levels, graded values present at every level, the same tile set at
  every level).

Added or changed:

- New section "The quick squeeze", with numeric limits a program can check: no
  word over 12 letters, no sentence over 20 words, an average sentence of 14
  words or fewer. They sit above the longest juice box passages in the 2.x
  examples (8 letters, 16 words, an average of 11.1 words).
- New section "What changed from 2.x".
- Steps 9 to 12 now make and check the one pass: draft it, cut it to the limits,
  source every figure, state the confidence. Step 13 is the gate, as before.
  Steps 1 to 8 are unchanged. The standard is still thirteen steps.
- The machine gate is nine checks for one pass: every figure has a source or is
  declared derived and says how; the confidence is stated; plain words; short
  sentences; no dashes; the cost line is present; the twelve tiles in order; no
  evidence block without a result; and the What's next rules. The old seventh
  check, the model name in What's next, is now check 9.
- The verdict tile carries its confidence.
- New non-normative section "Using the quick squeeze in a product".
- The stated conformance line is now "conforms to K13 3.0.0, quick squeeze,
  gate checks 1 to 9".

Migrating from 2.x.

- A report written at six levels: keep the juice box text as the pass, check it
  against the limits and add the confidence sentence if it has none. Drop the
  other five passes from the report, or keep them as product features outside
  the standard.
- A product with a level picker: the picker is yours to keep or retire. K13
  asks only for the quick squeeze. A stored level that is `juice_box` maps to
  the quick squeeze being on. The other five stored values have no K13 meaning;
  what a product does with them is its own decision. Do not rename a stored
  value or the box it lives in to match this document.
- A checker that read `k13-levels.json`: the file is gone. Read the version from
  `K13.md` or from `versions.json`.
- A gate that ran checks 1 to 6 of 2.x: re-run it against checks 1 to 9 above.
  A report that passed the 2.x gate has no guarantee of passing the new limits.

### 2.2.0

Wording, the long name, and the default level. A 2.1.0 report conforms to 2.2.0
unchanged, because every report already carries all six levels. No step number,
count, level name, reserved name or icon changes. One implementation rule
changes: the default level, described below.

- The long name is "Ai General Response Standard". The short name K13 is
  unchanged. It was "AI Response Standard".
- The default response level is now juice box. It was energy drink. Step 9 now
  carries the default rule that step 11 carried, and the new section "The
  default level" states what it means for pickers, reports, agents and saved
  choices. A remembered pick still wins, and no stored level identifier is
  renamed, so no saved choice resets.

- The six levels are named "response levels": the same answer, adjusted to the
  reader's preference. "Learning level" and "reading level" are retired as
  names for them, because the levels do not teach and do not rate the reader.
- Section "The learning level ladder" is now "The response levels", and "How to
  hit any learning level" is now "How to hit any response level". The anchors
  `#the-learning-level-ladder` and `#how-to-hit-any-learning-level` become
  `#the-response-levels` and `#how-to-hit-any-response-level`. The "Rule
  carried here" labels at steps 9 to 12 now read "(response level)".
- New heading "The standard at a glance" over the opening paragraph that sat
  under "Canonical text and mirrors".
- Step 7 pointed at `docs/command-templates.md`, a path that is not in this
  repository. It now points at the "Command template library" section.
- Plainer wording in the opening, scope and levels sections: repeated
  explanations removed and each term defined once. No requirement was changed
  by this.
- "One fact, six ways" now states the version numbers its closing paragraph
  says every pass names.
- "One fact, six ways" is rewritten so it passes machine gate checks 1 and 2
  itself. It had a figure at two levels only ("four repositories") and a
  confidence statement at one level only, so by its own gate it was not clean.
  Every pass now carries the same four figures, the same verdict, the same
  confidence and the same provenance. The package's tests read the six passes
  out of this document and run checks 1 and 2 on them.
- New section "Level identifiers": a machine-readable identifier for each
  level (`juice_box` and so on), as a recommendation, with the 1.1.0 exception written
  down: soda pop persists as `vending_machine`. The stored legacy box name
  `reading_level_prefs` is untouched. Names and identifiers stay English;
  labels may be localized.
- A PASS is defined once, as the written text of one answer at one response
  level. Both "pass" and "level" stay normative.
- Every rendered glyph must have a text alternative: the level name, never the
  Unicode character name. How a screen reader reads a level is stated.
- New subsection "Test vectors": a passing and a failing six-level vector per
  machine gate check, shipped in `@randomknights/k13`. A vector illustrates a
  check and the numbered list wins on disagreement.
- New subsection "Stating conformance": an optional one-line statement,
  "conforms to K13 2.2.0, six response levels, gate checks 1 to 6".
- New data file `k13-levels.json`, generated from this document at site build
  time, so the ladder has one source.
- The package checker implements machine gate checks 1, 2 and 3 for the passes
  of a report, and a glyph text alternative check for HTML. Checks 1 to 6 of
  the gate itself are unchanged; this is implementation, not a new rule.
- Counts of the capitalized normative keywords, each counted on its own with
  "must not" apart from "must": must not 2 (was 1), must 3 (was 1), should 3
  (was 1), may 3 (was 2). The additions are one must (glyph text alternative),
  one must not (renaming a stored value), one must (names stay English), two
  should (identifiers, the legacy mapping) and one may (conformance statement).
- Deferred, not in this version: worked examples for each of the twelve tiles
  (not just the finding sentence). They risk reading as a detail ladder and are
  a large body of text; this is recorded here so the question is not lost.
- Kept as is by owner decision: the phrase "deep dive" in the definition of a
  response level, the education grade labels in the ladder table, and juice box
  as the default level.

### 2.1.0

Additive. A 2.0.0 report conforms to 2.1.0 unchanged, and no rule changes.

- New section "One fact, six ways": one real fact written at all six normative
  levels, so an implementer can see the reading ladder and the detail ladder
  side by side rather than inferring the difference from the prohibition. The
  rule it illustrates is unchanged. The example was written because the field
  drift 2.0.0 describes kept recurring in implementations that had read the
  rule and agreed with it.
- New section "Canonical text and mirrors", and a "Canonical text" line in the
  header. This file is the one authoritative text; every other copy is a mirror
  with no authority, a mirror states the version it carries, and the published
  artifacts a mirror can be checked against are named. Nothing here is new
  policy: the repository already served the canonical text and the version data
  at fixed addresses. What is new is that the standard says so in its own text,
  so a mirror carries the statement that it is a mirror.

### 2.0.0

BREAKING. A 1.1.0 report with four levels does not conform to this version.

- All SIX levels are normative. Loose leaf and yerba mate are promoted from
  RESERVED to required, they carry step numbers 12.1 and 12.2, and they get the
  same definition treatment the other four get. Every "four passes" in this
  document is now six.
- The ladder is stated to be a READING-LEVEL ladder: all six passes carry the
  same facts, figures, verdicts and confidence, and an implementation that adds
  figures as the level rises has built a detail ladder, which fails step 13 by
  construction.
- New section "Scope: when K13 applies". K13 is for work that produces a
  report. Applying the thirteen steps to a one-line answer, a conversational
  reply or a tool call is a misapplication, not a stricter reading.
- New section "The machine gate": the six checks an implementation runs before
  a K13 report ships, and the rule that a gate which has never failed is not a
  gate.
- New section "The report template": the ordered twelve-tile list a conforming
  report carries, plus a pointer to the reference templates now in this
  repository at `templates/`.
- Two of those tiles are new. "How it fits" says what plan or lane the work
  belongs to, what it unblocks, and what preceded it. "What's next" carries the
  role, the effort and a paste-ready command for the continuation, never a model
  name unless a published mapping is cited, and "No next step, this work is
  complete" is always a valid answer.
- New section "Structural invariance": the report shell is identical at every
  level and only the register changes. The 1.1.0 template taught the opposite by
  putting the checks table, the query block and the open questions at one level
  each, which is the mechanism behind the detail-ladder drift observed in the
  field.
- New section "Evidence blocks": every command or query appears with its real
  rendered result, results are never fabricated or quietly shortened, and
  evidence is invariant across all six levels.
- The dash rule at step 13 now forbids "--" as a dash surrogate, and names the
  replacements: a comma, a colon, or a new sentence.
- Step 11 names energy drink as the level a picker opens on when the reader has
  not chosen one.
- The step numbering is restated rather than extended: the standard is still
  thirteen steps, and the two new passes are numbered inside the step 12
  drafting block.

### Migrating from 1.1.0

A 1.1.0 report has four levels. To conform to 2.0.0 it adds a loose leaf pass
and a yerba mate pass carrying the same facts, the same figures, the same
verdicts and the same confidence as the other four. Nothing in the four
existing passes has to be rewritten: if they already agreed with each other,
they already agree with the two new ones once the new ones are written from the
same facts. An implementation that treated loose leaf and yerba mate as
reserved names now writes them.

that states it conforms to K13 1.1.0 is making a true statement about 1.1.0.

### 1.1.0

- Level 2 is renamed from "vending machine" to "soda pop". The storage
  identifier behind it is deliberately NOT renamed in implementations that
  already persist a reader's choice: renaming it would silently reset every
  saved level. The name is what the reader sees; the identifier is not.
- Two reserved expert levels are added above black coffee, loose leaf and
  yerba mate. They are reserved names and glyphs, not reading levels, and they
  carry no step number. The standard remains thirteen steps.
- Each of the four passes now names its icon by Unicode codepoint, so the
  icons are normative while this document stays ASCII.
- The opening line is clarified: thirteen steps every time does not mean
  thirteen units of work every time. It already said so further down, and now
  it says so where a reader meets it.
- The command template library section no longer names private repositories,
  an internal collection path or an internal publish page. The normative
  content, the field list and the one-canonical-copy requirement, is kept.
